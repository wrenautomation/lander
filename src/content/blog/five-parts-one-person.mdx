Every company I look at has the same five parts. A two-person shop has them. So does a company of a thousand. What changes is how many people each part takes.

<Rack title="The five parts of a business" unit="running">

- **People**: hiring, pay, who does what
- **Infrastructure**: the computers, accounts and tools it all runs on
- **Admin and finance**: books, contracts, invoices, tax
- **Delivery**: making the product and getting it to the customer
- **Sales and marketing**: finding the people who need it, and getting them to buy

</Rack>

I run Wren with no staff, so all five are mine. I can't do that by hand. So I build software for each part, and I run it on my own company first.

## I'm my own first customer

Before I sell a system to anyone, it runs Wren. Take Meta ads. Before one ad goes out, the account has to be set up and warmed up. Then I research who the ad is for. Then I write down what I'm testing: how aware is this person of the problem, what should the ad ask them to do, and where does the click land.

Each of those is a job someone does every time. So each one becomes a piece of software, and it breaks on my money before it ever touches a client's.

## What counts as busy work

<Note title="Busy work, the way I use the word">
Something you do often, that takes a lot of time, and that doesn't build a skill you want. Editing video is busy work for me. For someone who wants to get good at editing, it isn't.
</Note>

That's why I don't automate everything. If a task is teaching me something I need, I keep doing it myself.

## Where AI helps and where it doesn't

AI is very good at writing software, because software has a clear test: it runs or it doesn't. It's much weaker where there's no clear test, like coming up with ideas or writing copy that sells.

<Compare before="AI does it well" after="Stays with me">

- Writing and fixing the code for each part
- Doing the same steps the same way every time
- Turning one draft into many formats

---

- Deciding who a product is for
- The idea behind each post
- The last edit on anything a person reads

</Compare>

That's why I spend my own learning time on sales and marketing. Software keeps getting cheaper to build. Knowing what to build, and how to sell it, hasn't, at least not yet.
