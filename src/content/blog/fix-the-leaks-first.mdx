When a company wants more leads, the first idea is usually more ads. I look first at the leads and customers they already have. Most of the time the cheaper win is to stop losing them.

These are the five places I check, in order.

<Leaks title="Five leaks to fix first" unit="sealed">

- **Speed to lead**: answering a new inquiry while they're still looking
- **Reactivation**: going back to past customers and old leads
- **Milestone upsells**: offering the next thing when it makes sense
- **Onboarding**: making the first weeks easy, so new customers stay
- **Churn detection**: noticing a customer drifting before they leave

</Leaks>

## Speed to lead

In 2011, Harvard Business Review audited 2,241 US companies. Each got a web inquiry, and the researchers timed the reply.

<Stat n="23" suffix="%">
of the companies never replied at all. Source: [HBR, 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/03/the-short-life-of-online-sales-leads).
</Stat>

Only 37% replied within an hour. And the companies that did were nearly 7 times as likely to qualify the lead as the ones that waited even an hour longer.

So this is the first week I build for a new inquiry:

<Timeline title="A new lead's first week">

- **Under 60 seconds**: a text goes out
- **2 minutes**: a call
- **Day 1**: first follow-up
- **Day 3**: second follow-up
- **Day 7**: last follow-up

</Timeline>

All of it stops the moment they reply, book a time, or text STOP.

## Reactivation

People who already bought from you, or already asked, know who you are. A short note asking if they still need help costs almost nothing to send.

## Milestone upsells

When a customer hits a milestone, like a finished project or a first result, that's when the next offer makes sense. Earlier, it reads as a push. Months later, they've moved on.

## Onboarding and churn

The last two are about keeping the customers you have. Onboarding is the first weeks: does the new customer know what happens next, and does it happen? Churn detection is spotting a customer drifting away, and reaching out before they're gone.

<Stat n="5" suffix="%">
higher customer retention raised profits by 25% to 95%, in research by Bain's Frederick Reichheld. Source: [HBR, 2014](https://hbr.org/2014/10/the-value-of-keeping-the-right-customers).
</Stat>

The same piece puts a new customer at 5 to 25 times the cost of keeping one you already have.
